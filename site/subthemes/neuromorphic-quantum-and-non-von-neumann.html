Different physical systems require different definitions of a correct answer

This grouping combines eight papers, but it does not describe one computing architecture. Some propose circuits whose electrical or optical behavior performs part of a calculation.

Others generate classical control hardware, maintain a quantum device, compile error-correction circuits, or simulate superconducting gates. Their common concern is the relationship between an abstract operation and a physical implementation.

Their correctness requirements are not interchangeable.

A numerical solver can permit a stated approximation error. A cryptographic hash must implement its specified discrete function.

A quantum controller must issue the right signal at the right time. An error-correction schedule must preserve the intended checks while respecting device constraints.

A simulator must predict relevant physical behavior well enough to support its claimed use. Calling all of these systems “non-von Neumann” does not explain those differences.

Three full manuscripts were inspected for this pass: RISC-Q, NeuroPDE, and the NEAT PDF linked by an author's publication list. NEAT's PDF still has anonymous-author and placeholder publisher metadata; the author-hosted association supports its identity, not equivalence to a final published version.

Four other DAC papers remain description-backed. The ICCAD qudit paper remains supported by institutional and author abstracts.

Start with the computation, before choosing the hardware

A computing system accepts an input, represents it internally, changes that representation through a sequence of operations, and produces an output. To judge a proposed design, we must specify all four parts.

Saying that a circuit is analog, optical, or quantum does not yet tell us what input it accepts or what answer it must produce.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. Consider the difference between estimating a numerical value and calculating a hash. For an estimate, an output of 0.499 might be acceptable when the reference is 0.500, if the application permits an absolute error of 0.001.

For a hash, the requirement is equality with the specified discrete output; closeness of the underlying voltage is not the definition of correctness. These are illustrative requirements, not measurements from the papers.

They explain why the same physical noise can be tolerable in one computation and unacceptable in another.

Next ask what physical quantity carries the value. In a digital circuit, allowed voltage ranges encode discrete states.

In an analog circuit, the voltage or current itself can represent a numerical magnitude. A stochastic circuit can represent a transition probability through how frequently a device produces an outcome across repeated trials.

None of these choices eliminates representation: each defines a different relationship between physical behavior and the value the algorithm uses.

Then identify the operation that the device performs. A resistance can weight a current, but a complete numerical algorithm may also need to select inputs, accumulate values, apply a nonlinear rule, repeat a step, and decide when to stop.

If only the weighting operation moves into a new circuit, the other operations remain part of the system. Their cost can determine whether the complete calculation becomes faster.

Finally, follow the output back to the requirement. A numerical result needs an error measurement.

An exact discrete calculation needs a reference-output check. A controller needs a correctly timed signal, not merely the correct instruction in memory.

A device model needs agreement with the physical quantities it claims to predict. This sequence—input, representation, operation, output check—is the basis for comparing the branches below.

It is conceptual analysis, not a claim that all eight papers implement the same process.

Why a faster component may not produce a faster application

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. Suppose an illustrative computation spends 30 milliseconds preparing input, 60 milliseconds in the operation being accelerated, and 10 milliseconds reading and checking the result. Its total is 100 milliseconds.

Making the middle operation ten times faster reduces it to 6 milliseconds, so the new total is 46 milliseconds: the application improves by about 2.17 times, not ten times. If the new hardware also requires 20 milliseconds of additional conversion, the total becomes 66 milliseconds and the improvement falls to about 1.52 times.

These numbers are a worked example, not a paper result.

The same accounting applies when the extra cost is repeated sampling, device programming, signal conversion, calibration, or schedule compilation. Preparation performed once can be shared across many uses, but only if the prepared state remains valid.

A comparison therefore needs both the cost per use and how often preparation must be repeated. This is why the individual paper discussions separate an internal operation from the complete task.

Physical numerical computation: use device behavior, then account for its error

A differential equation describes how a quantity changes. A partial differential equation can involve changes across several independent variables, such as position and time.

To obtain a numerical answer, a solver also needs an initial state, boundary conditions, and a rule for representing the unknown values. The same equation with different boundaries is a different computational problem.

Digital arithmetic approximates these quantities using finite numerical representations and finite operations. Analog circuits instead allow a continuously varying electrical quantity to represent part of the state.

That does not make the answer exact. Device variation, noise, finite settling time, limited dynamic range, and input/output conversion can all affect it.

A faster internal circuit is useful only if the complete output still meets the required error level.

NeuroPDE: perform probabilistic transitions with stochastic devices

The missing connection: how can random choices calculate an answer?

A numerical solver starts with a rule relating unknown values, together with enough specified values or other conditions to determine a solution. Random choices are useful only when their average produces the quantity required by that rule.

Randomness is not a replacement for formulating the problem.

Here is a deliberately small mathematical example, not a NeuroPDE experiment. Consider five equally spaced positions numbered 0 through 4. The values at the ends are fixed: position 0 has value 0, and position 4 has value 100. At each interior position, require the unknown value to equal the average of its two neighbors. Writing u1 for the value at position 1 gives:

u1 = (0 + u2) / 2
u2 = (u1 + u3) / 2
u3 = (u2 + 100) / 2

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. The solution is 25, 50, and 75 at positions 1, 2, and 3. Substitution checks it: for example, (25 + 75) / 2 = 50. This is the discrete steady diffusion equation with fixed end values and no internal source. “Discrete” means we represent the domain by these selected positions; “steady” means the required values are no longer changing with time.

Now start a sampled path at position 1. At each interior position, choose left or right with equal probability.

Stop when the path reaches either end and record that end's value. One path returns either 0 or 100, which is usually not the answer 25.

The estimate comes from averaging the values returned by many paths starting at the same position.

Why should that average solve the equations? Let m1, m2, and m3 be the expected final values from the three interior starting positions. “Expected” means the probability-weighted average over possible outcomes. From position 1, half the first steps go straight to value 0; half go to position 2, whose expected final value is m2. Therefore m1 = (0 + m2) / 2. The same first-step reasoning gives the other two equations above. The expected endpoint values satisfy exactly the required equations. That is the connection between random paths and this numerical answer.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. This finite, unbiased walk eventually reaches an endpoint with probability one. Solving the equations also shows that a path starting at position 1 reaches the 100-valued end with probability one quarter. If a particular set of 100 paths reaches that end 27 times, its estimate is 27 × 100 / 100 = 27, an absolute error of 2 from the reference 25. The 27 outcomes are illustrative, not observations or a guaranteed count.

Changing the equation can require different transition probabilities, accumulated contributions along the path, or different stopping rules. The example explains the basic connection; it is not the paper's complete formulation and does not show that every partial differential equation has this simple endpoint estimator.

What changes when a circuit makes the random choice?

An ordinary implementation can store the current position, generate a pseudorandom number, select a transition, update the position, and record a contribution. Pseudorandom numbers come from a deterministic algorithm with a changing internal state.

Their suitability depends on the statistical requirements of the computation, not on whether they are physically random.

NeuroPDE instead represents position by an active circuit neuron. Its ferroelectric tunnel-junction resistance sets a voltage that controls magnetic tunnel-junction switching probability.

The activation cycle reads the current neuron, attempts neighboring activation, stops writing after a switch, and resets the original neuron. Section III, PDF pages 3–4, Figures 3–6.

Here “neuron” names a circuit element representing a state. It does not mean the solver learns the answer from examples.

A magnetic tunnel junction has a resistance related to the relative magnetic orientation of two layers. A ferroelectric device retains an electrically programmable polarization state that can change its resistance.

The computational use of these devices depends on the relationship between the stored setting and the resulting transition probabilities.

Why must the old state reset? A single walker has one current position.

Leaving the old position active while also activating the new position would no longer represent that same single-walker process. Why stop competing activation?

Activating both destinations would likewise change the process being simulated. These requirements follow from the representation; circuitry that enforces them is part of the computation, not optional supporting detail.

Hardware-intrinsic randomness means the physical device supplies a random outcome. It does not mean every physical setting supplies the probability the equation needs.

The relevant check is the measured frequency of each valid transition under the actual programming and operating conditions, including invalid or simultaneous state activations.

Separate sampling error from an incorrectly implemented probability

Return to the five-position example. If the hardware moves right more often than left, it will reach the 100-valued boundary more often.

More paths then make the estimate more consistent around the answer to the wrong transition rule. Repetition reduces uncertainty about that biased result; it does not restore the intended equal-probability process.

For independent paths with unchanged outcome variance, the standard deviation of their mean decreases in proportion to 1 / sqrt(N), where N is the number of paths. Consequently, halving this sampling uncertainty requires about four times as many paths. Correlation between paths can invalidate that simple calculation. Device mismatch and a fixed error in a programmed probability are different issues from independent sampling noise.

Discretization is another separate approximation. A perfectly implemented random walk on a coarse grid can accurately solve the grid equations while remaining an inadequate approximation to the continuous problem.

An evaluation must identify whether it is measuring circuit behavior, error against the discrete solution, or error against the original continuous solution.

The evaluation uses SPICE (a circuit simulator that computes voltages and currents in detail) circuit simulations and Python system simulation, with a 45 nm generic process kit at 300 K. The reported 10 ns and 1.451 pJ concern the paper's single-walk operation, not a complete PDE (partial differential equation: an equation for how a quantity spreads in space and time) solution.

Equation 10 defines squared error from the analytical result, despite calling it variance. Temperature-induced drift is outside the study. Sections IV–V, PDF pages 4–8; Equation 10 and Figure 12 on PDF page 7.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. Squared error and statistical variance answer different questions. If a computed value is 27 and its reference is 25, its squared error is (27 − 25)² = 4. To estimate variance, repeat the computation and measure the spread around its own average. A method that repeatedly returns 27 can have small variance while remaining systematically wrong. The units of squared error are also squared; a threshold for it is not automatically a threshold for relative or percentage error.

A circuit simulator computes electrical behavior from component models. A system simulator then evaluates the larger algorithm under modeled behavior.

Neither is a measurement of a fabricated complete solver. The model can test a design idea, but its conclusions inherit the accuracy and range of the component models and the way circuit results enter the system simulation.

How to compare complete solvers fairly

Start with the same equation, domain, boundary conditions, output locations, and error requirement. Count how many paths and transitions are required, not just the cost of one primitive operation.

Include programming the device states, initializing paths, detecting and recording outcomes, accumulating the estimate, and checking the result. Account for concurrency explicitly; multiplying every operation by its individual latency (the wait for one result) can overestimate elapsed time when operations overlap.

Then repeat enough complete runs to distinguish bias from sampling spread. Vary the grid resolution separately from the path count.

Compare with an appropriate digital solver at the same accuracy target, including a deterministic numerical method when it is suitable; a comparison only against other random-walk hardware does not establish the best way to solve the equation.

The contribution under investigation is a cheaper physical implementation of specified probabilistic transitions. Establishing a faster complete numerical solution requires the additional accounting above. The evidence record retains the recovered source conditions and unresolved limits. No independent execution or new hardware result is claimed here.

Analog flow generation: integrating a learned model is not automatically solving the physical equations

First specify what is being generated

A fluid velocity field assigns a velocity to each position in a domain. In a two-dimensional representation, each sampled position may have two values: velocity along one spatial direction and velocity along the other.

A sequence of such fields can represent physical evolution over time. A generator may instead produce a single field, or samples conditioned on specified inputs.

Those are different tasks and need different tests.

A conventional physical solver applies governing equations together with initial conditions, boundary conditions, and material or flow parameters. A learned generator uses fitted parameters to produce outputs according to patterns learned from data.

It can be designed to respect physical constraints, but that property must be established rather than inferred from a fluid-related training dataset.

The paper proposes an MLP-Mixer model, an RRAM continuous-time neural ODE circuit, and noise-aware training with decoder retraining. Its description reports three turbulent-flow datasets and energy/latency reductions of 98.24%/99.99% against an A100.

Dataset identities, conditioning inputs, precision, physical realization, and complete comparison boundaries are not specified. Official DAC description.

The input/output distinction is therefore partly unresolved for this record. The following explanation describes the relevant computational structure without inventing its dataset, circuit dimensions, or operating conditions.

Two meanings of flow, and three meanings of time

Flow matching learns a rule that transports samples from an initial distribution toward a target distribution. A distribution describes which sample values are likely.

The learned rule tells each current sample how to change as generation progresses. Training fits that rule to target changes associated with a chosen probability path. Flow Matching for Generative Modeling.

The word “velocity” in this model can mean the rate at which a vector of internal values changes. It need not mean the physical velocity of a fluid particle.

The vector might eventually decode into fluid velocities, but the internal change rule and the final physical quantities are different objects.

It helps to give the three time variables separate names. Physical time describes the fluid's evolution, if that is part of the task.

Generation progress, often normalized from 0 to 1, describes movement through the learned transformation. Circuit time is how long the electrical implementation runs.

One generated field could describe one physical instant while requiring an entire generation trajectory and some elapsed circuit time to produce it.

Running that electrical trajectory faster does not mean that the system simulates more physical seconds per second unless the benchmark explicitly defines and measures that relationship. It first means the implementation takes less wall-clock time to execute the specified learned transformation.

What a neural ordinary differential equation supplies

An ordinary differential equation specifies a rate of change with respect to one independent variable. For a neural ODE, a neural network supplies that rate. In compact notation, dz/ds = f(z, s): z is the current internal state, s is generation progress, and f gives the proposed change at the current state and progress. “Latent” means this internal representation is not necessarily the final output representation. A decoder converts it into output values.

As a minimal mathematical example, start with z = 1 at s = 0 and use the constant change rule dz/ds = 2. At s = 1, the answer is 3. A simple digital calculation with four steps of size 0.25 produces 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3. It happens to be exact for this constant rule; a general state-dependent rule can introduce numerical integration error. This example is neither a trained flow-matching model nor a paper experiment.

There are two distinct sources of discrepancy. The learned function may not describe the desired transformation accurately.

Separately, the hardware or numerical solver may fail to follow that learned function accurately. A perfect implementation of the wrong learned function does not repair the model; a good model executed inaccurately does not preserve its software result.

Why a mixer architecture is relevant

An MLP, or multilayer perceptron, alternates learned weighted combinations with nonlinear transformations. The original MLP-Mixer separately mixes information across input positions and across feature channels.

A channel is one component of the representation at a position. This gives two kinds of learned interaction without requiring the attention mechanism used by a Transformer. Original MLP-Mixer paper.

Weighted combinations are candidates for resistive-array computation. That does not imply that the complete network consists only of matrix multiplication.

Nonlinear operations, offsets, normalization if present, state feedback, and final decoding must also be implemented or accounted for. The exact mixer adaptation in this fluid-generation paper remains a full-text question.

How an electrical circuit can implement a changing state

Resistive memory stores a programmable conductance, the reciprocal of resistance. In an ideal linear device, applying voltage V produces current I = G × V, where G is conductance. Summing currents from several devices can produce a weighted sum. Real devices and interconnects depart from this ideal, so the desired numerical function and the measured electrical function must be compared.

An integrator accumulates the resulting rate into a state. For a capacitor, dV/dt = I/C: current changes its voltage at a rate set by capacitance. With an ideal 1 nanofarad capacitor and constant 1 microampere current, the voltage changes by one volt in one millisecond. This elementary circuit calculation illustrates integration; it is not the paper's component sizing or speed.

To implement a neural ODE, the stored state influences the network's output, and that output drives changes to the state. This feedback closes the computational loop.

Scaling between numerical values and voltages, and between generation progress and circuit time, determines what electrical parameters are required. Finite voltage range and circuit bandwidth (how many bytes a link can move per second) limit the scaling; it cannot be increased without considering accuracy and stability.

A related paper by overlapping authors explicitly uses resistive-array neural computation with feedback integration for score-based generation. Its task and title differ from this DAC paper. Its device realization and benchmark numbers are not transferred here. Related circuit background, Introduction.

Continuous operation changes the error sources; it does not eliminate error

An analog circuit can evolve without the sequence of discrete arithmetic updates used in a digital integrator. It can still have programmed-weight error, offsets, noise, limited gain, finite response speed, and imperfect initialization. These affect the trajectory being computed.

For the constant-rate example, suppose the circuit implements 2.1 rather than the intended rate 2. Starting from 1 and running to generation progress 1 then produces 3.1 instead of 3.

Making the measurement more precise will reveal this difference, not remove it. For state-dependent dynamics, an early error can affect subsequent rates as well, so the final discrepancy need not equal a simple sum of independent errors.

Noise-aware training exposes the model to a chosen noise model during optimization. Decoder retraining changes how internal states are mapped to final outputs.

Either can improve results under the conditions used for training. Neither establishes robustness to arbitrary temperature changes, device aging, programming errors, or a different noise distribution.

Testing new devices and held-out operating conditions addresses a different question from fitting the training setup.

Evaluate the right kind of accuracy

For a deterministic prediction with a specified reference, pointwise velocity error can be meaningful. For a generator that intentionally produces multiple possible fields, a different sample need not be an incorrect sample.

Evaluation may need distributional statistics and diversity as well as agreement with physical constraints. A visually plausible field is not enough, but insisting that every valid random sample equal one reference is not generally the right requirement either.

The benchmark should state what is conditioned on and which outputs are valid under that condition. If it claims incompressible flow, test the relevant mass-conservation constraint; if it claims boundary-conditioned generation, test those boundaries.

If it claims physical time evolution, test temporal consistency and the specified dynamics. Do not require or claim properties the task never defines.

Separate software-model error from the extra error introduced by analog execution. Hold architecture and training data fixed when testing the hardware effect, then evaluate any noise-aware retraining as an additional intervention. New geometries, flow regimes, or boundary conditions require evidence of their own.

Read the reductions as ratios with unresolved boundaries

A 98.24% energy reduction means 1.76% of the comparison energy remains within the measured boundary. A 99.99% latency reduction means 0.01% of the comparison latency remains. Such rounded percentages cannot establish precise complete-system speedups when the reference durations and included operations are unavailable.

The comparison needs training and programming policy, input initialization, integration, conversion, decoding, and output handling. Costs paid once should be separated from costs paid for every sample, with the number of uses stated.

Identical quality targets matter: a fast inaccurate sample and a slower accurate sample do not establish an accuracy-matched acceleration.

Optical computation: light transport does not remove the representation problem

Optical circuits can combine signals while they propagate. The resulting intensity or phase must still represent the desired mathematical quantity.

Loss reduces signal strength, and uncertainty affects how reliably the output can be interpreted. The source, modulation, detection, conversion, control, and memory costs belong in an application comparison.

PhotoMT: accelerate hash-tree construction, not every part of a proof

What is the required output?

A cryptographic hash maps an input to a fixed-size output according to a specified algorithm. Different inputs can share an output because the output space is finite; the security requirement is that finding useful collisions should be computationally infeasible under the stated assumptions.

A Merkle tree hashes groups of values repeatedly to produce one root. An inclusion proof supplies the intermediate values needed to connect one entry to that root.

The verifier must already have an authenticated or otherwise trusted root; a supplied root is not trustworthy merely because it is a hash. A concrete tree and inclusion-proof specification: RFC 9162, Sections 2.1.1–2.1.3.

The tree does not itself hide its input values or establish a general privacy guarantee. Zero knowledge is an additional property of a proof protocol: the verifier should learn that an assertion holds without learning the private information used to establish it beyond what the assertion reveals.

A tree may be one component of such a protocol. Faster tree construction is consequently not the same measurement as faster complete proof generation.

Follow an eight-leaf construction

The following is a structural example, not PhotoMT's reported tree configuration or a cryptographic implementation. Assume eight leaf hashes L0 through L7 are already available. Let H(left, right) denote the specified internal-node hash, with unambiguous encoding and ordering. The required intermediate nodes are:

P0 = H(L0, L1)       P1 = H(L2, L3)
P2 = H(L4, L5)       P3 = H(L6, L7)
Q0 = H(P0, P1)       Q1 = H(P2, P3)
root = H(Q0, Q1)

There are seven internal hashes: four at the first level, two at the next, and one at the root. The four first-level hashes do not depend on one another, so hardware can potentially calculate them concurrently.

Q0 must wait for P0 and P1, and the root must wait for both Q0 and Q1. More arithmetic units do not remove these dependencies.

The example excludes the cost of computing the eight leaf hashes.

To check the inclusion of L2 at its stated position, a verifier needs L3, P0, and Q1. It computes P1 from L2 and L3, then Q0 from P0 and P1, then the root from Q0 and Q1.

It compares that result with the trusted root. The verifier performs three internal hashes without recomputing the whole tree.

Correct left/right ordering is essential; silently sorting pairs changes the specified construction.

Real schemes also specify how leaf hashes differ from internal-node hashes, how values are encoded, and how an incomplete last level is handled. Those rules are part of correctness.

The schematic above explains dependencies, not permission to substitute an arbitrary encoding into a deployed proof system.

What work does PhotoMT move into optics?

The official description assigns matrix-vector multiplication to photonic microring arrays and control-intensive work to digital circuits. It adds multi-subtree interleaving and an S-box bypass queue.

It reports 18.8–20.5× throughput (how much work is finished per second) over specialized ASIC (a chip made for one job) designs and energy-efficiency gains of three to five orders of magnitude over a CPU (the general-purpose processor that runs ordinary programs). Exact parameters and the full experimental accounting are unavailable. Official DAC description.

A matrix-vector multiplication produces several weighted sums. For example, one output may be 2 × x + 3 × y; another can use different coefficients with the same input values. Optical hardware can potentially perform such signal weighting and combination while electronic hardware handles sequencing and storage. Whether that division is beneficial depends on input representation, conversion, device control, and how much of the hash actually consists of the accelerated operation.

A microring resonator is a small closed optical path coupled to other optical paths. Its wavelength-dependent transfer behavior can be used in optical signal processing. Device background: Bogaerts and colleagues, Sections 1–2, PDF pages 1–2.

The PhotoMT description does not specify enough to reconstruct its complete numerical encoding, calibration procedure, or exact-output recovery. It would be incorrect to supply a familiar optical-neural-network implementation and label it as this paper's design.

Why approximate arithmetic is a central verification issue

Poseidon maps sequences of elements from a specified finite field to field-element outputs. The field and security parameters help determine the required configuration.

Its S-box is a nonlinear arithmetic transformation; a common supported choice is raising an element to the fifth power in the field. Poseidon authors' parameter and reference-implementation page.

For a small arithmetic illustration, use integers modulo the prime 7: the allowed values are 0 through 6, and an arithmetic result is reduced to its remainder after division by 7. With x = 4 and y = 5, the weighted sum 2 × x + 3 × y is 23, whose field value is 2. A different recovered integer, 24, has field value 3. This tiny field is not a secure Poseidon parameter choice or a PhotoMT experiment. It makes the exactness requirement explicit.

An internal analog signal need not itself be exact if a demonstrated decoding rule reliably recovers the exact discrete value. For instance, in an idealized direct-integer encoding, nearest-integer decoding tolerates an error strictly below one half of the spacing between adjacent encoded integers.

That statement depends on the encoding scale, range, rounding rule, and absence of other errors. It is not a claimed error bound for the optical engine.

The complete implementation must establish the corresponding bound or correction method for its actual representation. It must also account for finite range, accumulation, field reduction, and nonlinear operations.

A small average numerical error does not establish that every hash result is correct. A single wrong internal value can produce a root different from the reference and cause an otherwise valid proof to be rejected.

These are obligations of an exact arithmetic implementation, not evidence that PhotoMT fails them. The available description leaves the relevant mechanism and tests unresolved.

Why interleaving independent subtrees can help

In the eight-leaf example, parallelism decreases as computation approaches the root: four available hashes become two, then one. If a hardware pipeline can accept a new hash before an earlier one finishes, it still needs ready inputs for that new hash.

A dependent parent cannot be submitted before its children are available.

Work from another independent subtree or another tree can fill some of the resulting gaps. Interleaving therefore changes which ready work enters the hardware next; it does not remove the original dependencies.

The general tradeoff is extra live state: partial results, identifiers, and ordering information must remain available until each parent can be formed.

More interleaving is not automatically better. If storage or data transfer becomes the limiting resource, keeping more trees active can increase waiting.

A throughput comparison should identify tree size, number of concurrent trees, input arrival pattern, memory capacity, and whether it includes the initial filling and final draining of the hardware pipeline.

The name “S-box bypass queue” is not a sufficient implementation description. A bypass is correct only when it avoids work that the specified algorithm does not require on that path, or implements an equivalent transformation.

It cannot simply omit a required nonlinear step while claiming the same hash. Which data bypass which operation, and under which Poseidon rounds, remains a full-text question for this paper.

Read throughput and energy with the same boundaries

Throughput counts completed outputs per unit time under a sustained workload. Latency measures the elapsed time for a particular request.

Interleaving can improve the first without improving the second by the same factor. The throughput result also needs an output definition: hashes, complete trees, and complete proofs are different units of work.

Energy per output must include the components inside the claimed comparison boundary. For a photonic-electronic system, the relevant accounting questions include optical sources, signal conversion, electronic control, memory traffic, and device stabilization.

A comparison using only optical computation energy cannot be read as whole-system energy without additional evidence.

The description uses different comparators for throughput and energy efficiency. Combining those gains into a claim that one measured system simultaneously achieves both advantages over the same baseline (the existing method a new result is compared against) would exceed the source.

The implementation stage—modeled, synthesized, prototyped, or fabricated—also needs to be established rather than inferred from the word “experiments.”

What is recoverable now, and what remains to verify?

Fresh name and author searches found the official program entry but no matching full manuscript or artifact. The paper evidence record therefore remains description-backed. The external Poseidon and tree specifications explain the underlying requirements; they do not document PhotoMT's implementation.

A reproduction should first match full hashes and roots against a versioned reference with identical parameters and encodings. It should then test independent subtrees and dependent parent updates, including conversion limits and any retry or correction.

Finally, measure complete-tree latency, sustained throughput, and total energy at the same correctness requirement. Only a separate complete-proof experiment can establish how much this component changes proof-generation cost.

Bident: optimize how optical signals are combined

The required logic result comes before the loss score

An optical logic circuit represents logical values through physical optical signals. To implement a specified function, the output signals must remain distinguishable under the receiver's decision rule.

A smaller power loss is useful when it helps preserve that distinction or reduces the required input power. It is not, by itself, proof that the circuit computes the right Boolean function.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. For example, suppose an illustrative receiver treats output power above 0.5 units as logical 1. If a valid input pattern should produce 1 but reaches the receiver at 0.49, that implementation fails the specified decision rule.

Raising the result to 0.51 may fix this one nominal case, but it leaves only 0.01 units of margin. Device variation or input uncertainty can still matter.

These are hypothetical values, not Bident measurements or a proposed universal optical threshold.

Logic synthesis chooses an implementation of a required logical function. For optical logic, the physical signal behavior must constrain that choice. Otherwise, a logically valid arrangement of ideal gates may not produce reliably distinguishable signals after it is implemented.

Why tree structure changes the loss of each input

A binary combiner accepts two incoming paths and supplies an outgoing path. Combining more inputs requires several such elements.

A tree records which paths combine first and which combinations occur later. A leaf is an original input; an internal node is a combining operation; the root is the final output.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. Consider four inputs. A balanced arrangement combines inputs 1 and 2, combines inputs 3 and 4, then combines the two results.

Every input passes through two combining stages. An unbalanced arrangement can give one input a one-stage path, another a two-stage path, and the remaining two three-stage paths.

Both arrangements contain three internal combiners. Equal component count therefore does not imply equal paths or equal loss for each input.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. For a deliberately simplified single-active-input calculation, assume each traversed stage delivers 80% of the incoming power to the required output path, independent of signal level. Two stages deliver 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64 of the initial power; three deliver 0.8³ = 0.512; one delivers 0.8. The retained fractions multiply rather than adding. Giving an input a shorter path changes its delivered power even when no combiner is removed.

This calculation excludes simultaneous-input interference, wavelength dependence, reflections, and layout loss. When optical fields combine coherently, their relative phase can reinforce or cancel the resulting field; simply adding independently computed powers can then be wrong.

The toy calculation explains cumulative path attenuation, not the complete binary aggregate model claimed by Bident.

What Huffman optimization means—and what it does not prove here

For the standard binary weighted-path problem, each leaf has a nonnegative weight and a depth. The objective is the sum of weight × depth. Huffman's construction repeatedly combines the two smallest remaining weights and replaces them by their sum, producing an optimal tree for that objective. Its original application minimizes average encoded message length. Huffman's original paper, pages 1098–1101.

Quantum control is classical computation with strict physical timing

A quantum device does not directly execute a line of software. Classical electronics turn commands into signals, receive measurements, interpret them, and decide what happens next.

Control timing concerns both when a signal begins and how its phase evolves. Feedback delay includes the measurement, signal processing, decision, and delivery of the next command.

A processor's clock frequency describes internal cycles. It does not specify the complete feedback latency or prove that every programmed sequence meets its timing requirements. Similarly, supporting several output channels is not evidence of controlling the same number of independent qubits under every protocol.

At execution time, the resulting controller takes a program, signal parameters, and measurement data and produces control signals. Generating the controller is not the same task as running a quantum algorithm, and improving the generator does not by itself improve the accuracy of a quantum operation.

A field-programmable gate array, or FPGA (a chip whose circuits can be rewired after it is made), contains configurable logic and storage. Loading a design configures these resources into circuits that can operate concurrently.

This distinction explains why a design can combine ordinary software with dedicated timing hardware instead of asking software to perform every operation at the required instant.

Follow one measurement-dependent operation from start to finish

The following is a conceptual execution sequence, not a measured timing trace from the prototype. It makes the controller's responsibilities explicit.

  1. Software specifies a signal and when it should be emitted. The specification must reach the signal-generation circuit before that deadline.
  2. The circuit produces digital samples. A digital-to-analog converter, or DAC, converts those numbers into an electrical signal supplied to the physical system.
  3. A measurement returns an electrical signal. An analog-to-digital converter, or ADC (analog-to-digital converter: turns a voltage into a number), produces samples from it. Processing those samples produces a decision value; acquiring a signal and deciding what it means are separate operations.
  4. The decision selects the next operation. The selected operation must then reach the output circuit in time to be useful.

The delay of the complete feedback sequence includes acquisition, conversion, processing, decision, and output delivery. Improving only the decision step cannot remove delays in the others.

When stages overlap across repeated operations, throughput—the number completed per second—can improve without an equal reduction in the delay of one operation.

What a timed queue changes

A queue stores commands until they can be used. A timed queue additionally associates commands with their intended execution times.

Software can prepare a command early; timing circuitry releases it at the scheduled instant. This separates when software prepares work from when the physical signal must occur.

That separation does not make deadlines automatic. If the command arrives after its scheduled time, the queue cannot deliver it in the past.

If preparation produces commands more slowly than the output consumes them, the queue eventually empties. If preparation is too far ahead, finite queue capacity can fill.

Correct operation therefore depends on command arrival time, capacity, and consumption rate, not simply on the presence of a queue.

Memory-mapped input/output means that some processor addresses refer to hardware control registers rather than ordinary stored data. A write to one of these addresses changes a circuit setting or submits a command.

A read can retrieve a measurement result. This provides a software interface; the time required for the write to reach and affect the peripheral remains a hardware question.

Why preparing both alternatives can shorten a decision

The paper supports preloading two candidate gates into parallel timed queues and selecting between them through a control register after measurement. It contrasts this with a processor branch and explicitly notes a possible throughput cost from preloading. Section V-B, PDF page 8.

The general reasoning is a dependency change. Without advance preparation, work that depends on the outcome must wait until the outcome is available.

Preparing both possibilities moves some work before that point. Afterward, the remaining task is selection rather than preparation of the chosen alternative.

This is useful only to the extent that selection really is cheaper on the time-critical sequence.

It also means preparing an alternative that will not be used. Storage and command-delivery capacity are finite, so the extra work can reduce how many complete operations the system sustains.

There is no contradiction between shorter decision delay and lower throughput: one measures waiting along a particular dependency sequence; the other measures the continuing rate at which resources can serve repeated work.

Active calibration: decide what to measure and when to interrupt useful work

The control setting is not the physical result

A controller can issue the same command twice without causing exactly the same physical operation. The device and its surrounding electronics can change.

Calibration uses experiments to estimate which control settings currently produce the intended behavior. Characterization measures properties of the device; calibration uses such information to choose or update settings.

Neither is the same as merely storing the last setting that was used. Qiskit Experiments framework paper, “Summary” and “Statement of need,” PDF pages 1–2.

An experiment has an input setting, a procedure, measured outcomes, and an analysis that estimates a parameter or operation quality. The result should include uncertainty and the conditions under which it was obtained.

Applying a fitted setting is a separate step. Checking whether that setting actually meets the operating requirement is another.

Conflating these steps makes it possible to report a successful fit without establishing a successful physical operation.

The goal is not to find a permanent best number. It is to keep the device operating within its requirements while spending an acceptable amount of time on measurement and adjustment.

A measurement can be useful immediately and become outdated later. Repeating every experiment constantly reduces that risk but consumes resources that could otherwise execute useful work.

Why knowing a value includes knowing when it was measured

Worked example, with the numbers

Choosing a measurement is different from scheduling it

An uncertain model assigns possible outcomes to an unperformed experiment. Expected information gain estimates how much the observation would reduce that uncertainty, averaged over those possible outcomes.

Dividing by experiment time expresses a preference for informative measurements that are inexpensive to obtain. The expectation is model-dependent: it is not information already observed.

Bayesian updating changes the relative plausibility of possible parameter values using how likely the observed data would be under each value. For example, suppose two candidate states initially have equal probability.

A particular measurement outcome has probability 0.8 under the first state and 0.2 under the second. After observing that outcome, their updated probabilities are 0.8 and 0.2: multiply each prior probability by its outcome probability, then divide by the total.

This calculation depends on the assumed measurement model; it is not certainty that the first state is correct. Bayesian experiment selection asks which next observation would most help the decision before paying to obtain it.

For a simple illustration, an experiment expected to provide 0.8 bits of information in four seconds has an expected rate of 0.2 bits per second. Another expected to provide one bit in ten seconds has a rate of 0.1.

The first wins this particular ratio even though the second offers more total information. These hypothetical values explain the objective; they do not measure the paper's implementation or a real calibration experiment.

Here a bit measures uncertainty reduction, not a stored measurement's file size. In the special case of equally likely possibilities, narrowing four possibilities to two removes one bit of uncertainty.

With unequal probabilities or noisy observations, information gain must use the full probability distribution. The ratio above assumes those expected gains have already been estimated consistently.

Information alone is not operation quality. Resolving a harmless parameter very precisely may be less valuable than checking a poorly known parameter that determines whether a gate meets its requirement.

A good selection rule must connect uncertainty reduction to the device-level objective or constraints. Comparing information measures across parameters also requires a consistent probabilistic definition, not arbitrarily comparing their numerical variances when units and scales differ.

Once an experiment is selected, scheduling determines its start time, resource assignment, and interaction with other work. Reinforcement learning can learn a decision rule from the consequences of actions; its behavior depends on what information it observes and what reward it receives.

Integer optimization can enforce discrete choices and constraints for a specified model. Naming both methods does not establish how they coordinate, which has final authority, or what happens when they disagree.

Why fewer recalibrations can be a misleading result

The mean time between recalibrations measures an interval between maintenance events. It does not measure whether the device was healthy throughout the interval.

A policy that never recalibrates can make this interval very long while producing unusable operations. The interval becomes meaningful only with an operating-quality requirement and evidence about how long that requirement was satisfied.

A health indicator can detect that performance has changed without identifying which setting caused the change. It is also an estimate obtained from finite measurements.

A trigger therefore needs a rule for uncertainty, repeated evidence, and recovery. Using the same observations both to choose a change and to declare the change successful can overstate the benefit; an additional validation measurement may be needed.

Quantum compilation and simulation: distinguish design choices from device observations

Quantum error correction stores logical information across several physical qubits. Additional measurement qubits collect checks, called syndromes, without directly reading out the logical state.

A decoder uses those outcomes to infer an appropriate correction. Different gate schedules can implement the same checks while exposing the system to different error opportunities.

NEAT: choose placements and check schedules together

What must an error-correction check reveal—and not reveal?

Quantum error correction protects information by encoding it across several physical qubits. The encoded information is called a logical qubit.

A check measures a property shared by the valid encoded states, rather than directly measuring the unknown logical information. Additional qubits used to collect these checks are called ancillas.

The list of check results is the syndrome; a classical decoder uses it to infer a correction. A stabilizer code specifies compatible checks that leave its valid encoded states unchanged when there is no error. IBM's explanation of stabilizer codes.

Worked example, with the numbers

from the papers
  1. Consider an actual three-qubit bit-flip code as a small mathematical example, not a NEAT benchmark. Its two encoded basis states are 000 and 111; an unknown encoded quantum state can be a superposition of these two. The checks ask whether qubits 1 and 2 agree, and whether qubits 2 and 3 agree. Both valid basis states answer “agree” to both checks. Checking agreement therefore need not reveal which encoded basis state is present. IBM's repetition-code explanation.
  2. For example, a flip of the middle qubit changes 000 to 010 and 111 to 101. In either case, both adjacent pairs disagree. A flip of the first qubit changes only the first check; a flip of the third changes only the second. Under the explicit assumption of at most one bit flip and perfect checks, the two results identify which qubit needs correction. The calculation does not require learning whether the encoded state was 0, 1, or their superposition.

The assumptions matter. Two bit flips can produce the same check results as a different single flip.

A decoder that assumes one flip can then make the wrong correction. This small code also does not protect arbitrary phase errors, which change the relative phase of a superposition rather than simply exchanging 0 and 1.

The example explains why checks are useful, not why this code alone is sufficient for reliable general quantum computation. IBM's bit-flip-code tutorial.

For this example, a superposition is a weighted combination of the 000 and 111 quantum states. The weights determine more than the probabilities of reading those strings: their relative sign can affect later quantum operations. The equally weighted plus and minus combinations are different states, yet both pass the two agreement checks. Changing that relative sign is a concrete phase error that these checks cannot detect. Protecting against bit flips alone therefore leaves part of the encoded information unprotected.

Why the check becomes a placement and scheduling problem

A check is not an abstract question answered at no cost. Its ancilla must interact with the relevant data qubits, then be measured.

Those interactions are physical operations that take time and can introduce errors. If two requested operations need the same qubit at the same time, the device generally cannot execute them as independent two-qubit gates.

Operations on disjoint qubits can still conflict because they share control resources or produce unwanted interactions.

On a neutral-atom platform, individual trapped atoms carry the qubits. Moving atoms changes which pairs can interact.

A compiler must therefore output more than a reordered list of gates: it needs a placement and a physically permitted sequence of movements and operations. A valid ordering in the abstract circuit can be inconvenient or impossible in a particular geometry.

NEAT jointly models placement, check ordering, and motion. Its constraints cover required interactions, exclusive use of resources, ordering needed for logical correctness, and geometric feasibility. Sections 3–4, PDF pages 3–4.

The input is a specification of required checks plus an architecture model. The output is a schedule and mapping that satisfy the encoded constraints.

The compiler is classical software; it does not execute the checks or decode experimental measurements merely by producing that output. Whether the architecture model adequately represents the device is a separate validation question.

A constraint solver assigns values to variables while enforcing rules. For a scheduling problem, variables can specify a gate's time slot and an atom's position.

A rule can prohibit two gates from using the same qubit in one slot. Another can require a valid interaction geometry whenever a gate is scheduled.

An objective selects a preferred valid assignment, such as one with fewer layers or less movement.

An optimizer's result has several possible meanings. It may find a valid solution without proving it best, prove an optimum under its model, prove that the encoded problem has no solution, or stop before deciding.

Qudit simulation: predict interactions and leakage in a larger state space

Three levels change both representation and control

A qubit uses two selected quantum states as its computational basis, usually labeled 0 and 1. A qutrit uses three, labeled 0, 1, and 2.

Two qutrits therefore have nine joint basis states: each of the first system's three labels can be paired with each of the second's. Two qubits have four.

This enlarges the representable state space; it does not by itself establish faster execution of an application.

A superconducting transmon is a circuit with multiple quantum energy levels, not a physical object limited to exactly two states. Qubit operation chooses two levels for computation; qutrit operation deliberately uses a third.

The spacing between transitions affects whether control can address the intended change without driving unwanted changes. Original transmon design paper.

Using more levels changes what counts as an error. Occupation of level 2 is outside a two-level qubit's computational space, but inside a qutrit's.

A qutrit still has higher unwanted states outside its selected three-level space. A simulator must include enough physical levels to test unwanted transfer rather than eliminating the possibility by construction.

The output of a gate is more than a list of populations

A controlled-phase gate changes relative phases according to the joint state. A phase affects how components of a superposition combine in later operations.

A minus sign is one simple phase change. Unlike a bit flip, it need not change the probability of immediately measuring a particular basis label.

It must also state how leakage is handled. Conditioning on runs that remain in the computational space can conceal how frequently the system leaves that space unless the survival probability is reported separately.

Device parameters determine the energy levels and interactions available to the controller. A control waveform specifies how adjustable quantities vary during execution.

These are distinct layers of the calculation. Agreement in an energy-level calculation does not automatically imply an accurate noise model. A realistic noise model cannot correct a wrong control waveform. An optimizer can only optimize the behavior represented by the assembled model.

Why an interaction can produce a conditional phase

In a cross-Kerr description, one subsystem's state changes an energy contribution associated with the other. Because quantum phases accumulate according to energy and elapsed time, the joint state can acquire a phase that depends on both subsystem states.

A tunable coupler changes the effective interaction, allowing control over that accumulation.

For a purely illustrative constant interaction, define an angular rate chi so that the conditional phase magnitude is chi × duration. Doubling the rate and halving the duration produces the same phase in that simplified calculation. Real devices need not permit that substitution: changing the coupling can also change unwanted transitions, sensitivity to control error, and other phase contributions. Matching one accumulated phase is not a complete gate design.

Slow control and noise can favor different choices

Adiabatic control changes parameters sufficiently slowly relative to the relevant energy separations so that the system approximately follows the intended changing energy states. “Slowly” is relative to the device spectrum and path, not an absolute duration that works for every circuit.

Rapid variation can cause unwanted transitions; smaller gaps can make a particular path harder to follow.

A longer operation also leaves more time for relaxation or loss of phase coherence. Relaxation changes state populations; loss of coherence can destroy the relative phase information needed by later operations.

Making the pulse longer can reduce one error source while increasing another. Optimizing that tradeoff requires both a dynamical model and a specified noise model.

Pulse shape matters as well as duration. Two pulses with the same endpoints and total time can pass through sensitive regions at different rates. A search over duration alone and a search over full waveforms are different optimization problems and require different budgets.

Durable connections and bounded long-term hypotheses

The shared first principle is that representation determines the validation burden. A stochastic transition must have the right probability.

An analog integrator must follow a sufficiently accurate trajectory. Optical hashing must recover exact discrete values.

A controller must respect timing. A compiler must preserve logical meaning while accounting for motion.

A simulator must predict the physical quantities used to justify decisions.

One hypothesis is that device-aware compilation and calibration will become increasingly coupled: schedules depend on device conditions, while operating schedules affect which conditions need monitoring. Evidence would require a combined experiment showing improved useful computation at a fixed error requirement after charging for sensing, recompilation, and calibration.

RISC-Q, active calibration, and NEAT supply related mechanisms, not such a combined demonstration.

The dependency is concrete. A compiler needs estimates of gate duration, error, and allowed interactions to choose a schedule.

Calibration updates those estimates. A controller must then execute the selected operations with the assumed timing.

If calibration changes a relevant parameter, an earlier schedule's predicted quality may no longer apply. A simulator can help evaluate candidate changes, but only within its validated range.

None of these four steps can establish the others merely by succeeding at its own task.

A combined test would run the same workload and required output quality under two policies: one using a fixed schedule with its specified maintenance policy, and another allowing recalibration and rescheduling.
