Building a usable stack, not just a compact arrangement

A three-dimensional chip divides circuitry among layers and connects those layers vertically. This adds choices that do not exist in a single-layer placement: which layer owns each function, where a signal crosses layers, and whether the connection locations fit the bonding structure.

A shorter geometric distance is only useful if the resulting circuit can be connected, clocked, powered, cooled, and manufactured.

This subject's six records mainly concern construction of that physical implementation. The neighboring stacking and thermal-management article mainly concerns temperature prediction and operating policies. These labels overlap; they are retained to make the existing classification reviewable, not to assert that they are separate established fields.

Early partitioning and detailed placement make different commitments

Partitioning chooses which circuitry belongs on each layer. Placement assigns locations within those layers.

An early decision sees a less detailed design but can avoid expensive changes later. A late decision sees more realistic wire and cell information, but much of the structure is already fixed.

RTL-3D chooses layers using timing estimates before synthesis, the step that turns a behavioral hardware description into a network of implementation cells. The conceptual risk is estimation error: a path judged important early may change when logic is transformed and actual wires are introduced.

The captured record gives no timing, link-count, or implementation results. The appropriate test is whether early choices remain useful after the same complete implementation flow, including any corrective work.

RTL-3D: early tier partitioning is a prediction problem before it is a placement result

What tier partitioning decides

A 3D IC divides a design among physical tiers. Tier partitioning assigns selected logic or registers to those tiers and determines which signals must cross between them.

This decision happens before detailed cell placement and routing, so the exact wire shapes, buffer locations, parasitics and congestion are not yet known. The partitioner must estimate whether a proposed assignment will produce acceptable timing after those later steps.

At RTL, the input describes operations and storage relationships rather than final implementation geometry. Synthesis can change the number and structure of gates, duplicate logic, choose different cells and alter the paths that dominate timing.

A pre-synthesis predictor therefore does not directly measure final delay. It estimates a property of a future implementation, and its usefulness depends on whether the estimate remains informative after synthesis, placement, routing and extraction.

What the reported method changes

The captured description combines a Transformer-based pre-synthesis timing-analysis model with differentiable register tier partitioning. The timing model predicts timing-related information from the RTL representation.

The partitioning method treats register-to-tier decisions as differentiable during optimization, so timing feedback can influence the assignment instead of being used only after a discrete partition has already been chosen.

“Differentiable” means that the optimizer can use a computed change in its objective to adjust a continuous representation of a decision. A real register cannot finally occupy a fractional tier, so the method must eventually convert the relaxed decision into a discrete assignment and implement it.

That conversion can change timing and cross-tier traffic. The claimed feedback loop is therefore a design strategy; it is not by itself proof that the pre-synthesis prediction is accurate or that the final partition is legal under bonding, routing and thermal constraints.

The source reports 59.3% WNS (worst negative slack: how late the single worst timing path is) reduction and 75.4% TNS (total negative slack: how late all the failing timing paths are, added up) reduction against state-of-the-art methods and describes these as signoff timing improvements. WNS is the most negative timing slack (time to spare before a signal would arrive too late) under a stated convention; TNS aggregates negative slack across violating endpoints.

Percentage reductions need the baseline (the existing method a new result is compared against) and signed-slack convention. If the baseline values, signs, endpoint sets or timing corners are not shown, the direction and practical size of the claimed improvement cannot be independently reconstructed from the headline percentages.

The evaluation that would establish early partitioning value

Report the RTL designs, synthesis settings, technology libraries, number of tiers, vertical-link limits and training/test split. Measure predictor error before partitioning and compare predicted timing with post-synthesis and post-route timing on designs not used to fit the model.

For each partition, report absolute WNS, TNS, violating endpoints, cross-tier connection count, area, power, temperature, routing success and runtime through signoff. Compare the same designs with timing-oblivious and post-synthesis partitioners under equal implementation effort, and account for the cost of prediction, relaxation, discrete legalization and any repair after routing.

Integrated timing-driven placement: a 3D timing estimate must include the physical connection model

Why ordinary placement models are insufficient

Face-to-face hybrid bonding puts two dies in opposing orientations and connects them through die-to-die bonding locations. A placer must choose each cell or module's tier and in-tier position, then ensure that signals crossing tiers can reach legal bonding resources.

A shorter 2D distance is not enough: a cross-tier signal also depends on alignment, bonding density, parasitic capacitance/resistance, congestion, and the receiving tier's available connections.

Timing closure means that every required path meets its arrival constraint after implementation effects are included. Total negative slack (TNS) sums timing violations under a chosen convention; worst negative slack (WNS) records the most severe violation.

A reduction factor for TNS or WNS can be misleading when the baseline value is close to zero or when the signs and absolute values are not reported. Ratios must be accompanied by the underlying slack values.

What the integrated framework changes

The source describes a true-3D static-timing-analysis engine with more accurate parasitic modeling, a sample-based 3D wirelength model with hybrid weighting for global placement, and Lagrangian-based detailed placement. The timing model is used while placement is being optimized, so timing is not only checked after the geometric arrangement is chosen.

The sample-based wirelength model estimates connection cost without enumerating every final route; hybrid weighting combines placement objectives, but the description does not specify the weights or their calibration.

Lagrangian detailed placement treats constraints through penalties or multipliers while refining legal positions. This can coordinate timing, tier assignment, alignment and inter-tier connectivity, but a penalty model is not the same as a completed physical check.

The final result must still be routed and analyzed with the actual bonding and parasitic rules. Thermal consequences also remain separate: moving logic to improve timing can change power density and heat paths.

The official description reports 30–1002× TNS reduction and 3–14× WNS reduction over state-of-the-art placers. It does not provide absolute slack values, benchmark identities, process and bonding conditions, corner coverage, runtime, routed timing, alignment tolerance, thermal results, or the meaning of each range endpoint.

The source therefore supports an integrated timing-driven placement direction, not a verified timing-closure or universal 3D-placement result. Source: official DAC description and captured record.

Dense packing and programmable connections

POLT: fitting hard and soft 3D modules is a geometric problem before it is a chip problem

What a 3D floorplan must represent

A floorplan assigns each module a position and shape on one or more dies. A hard module has a fixed outline.

A soft module has a target area but can change its aspect ratio or boundary. A rectilinear shape has boundaries aligned with perpendicular directions rather than arbitrary curves.

A 3D floorplan adds die-to-die interfaces and alignment constraints between modules or connection regions on different dies.

The geometric objective is to place modules without overlap, satisfy target areas and alignment, and reduce estimated wirelength. A whitespace-free result means the chosen floorplan regions are packed without unused geometric regions under the model.

It does not mean that all physical space is removable: routing tracks, power delivery, thermal paths, keep-out regions, bonding pads, mechanical limits, and manufacturing tolerances may require space that a geometric packing model does not include.

What POLT changes

POLT uses a two-stage process. First, a force-directed method places hard modules and determines spatial relationships for soft modules while respecting constraints.

The force calculation is a placement heuristic: it moves objects according to the selected objective and constraints; it does not itself produce a legal routed design. Second, pressure-driven orthogonal Laguerre–Voronoi tessellation partitions the soft-module region into rectilinear shapes.

The tessellation is intended to match target areas with small error while preserving the relationships established in the first stage.

This separation matters. The first stage determines where regions belong and how they relate.

The second stage determines the exact rectilinear boundaries and area allocation. A shape can satisfy its target area while creating an unfavorable boundary for routing or a poor location for a die-to-die interface.

The method therefore needs downstream checks after tessellation, not only geometric area checks.

The official description reports an average 5.6% HPWL reduction on 3D floorplans with alignment constraints, 98% area-error reduction versus existing rectilinear floorplanners, and less than 0.16% area error across stated 2D and 3D stacking scenarios. HPWL is a placement estimate, not routed wirelength, delay, power or thermal behavior.

The description does not identify benchmarks, runtime, routing feasibility, interface capacity, process rules, or the denominator for area-error reduction. Source: official DAC description and captured record.

The evaluation that would establish physical usefulness

Report overlap, target-area error, alignment violations, HPWL, routed wirelength, congestion, die-to-die connection count, timing, power-delivery feasibility, temperature, runtime, and repair cost. Compare hard/soft and alignment-constrained cases separately.

Run the same placement through detailed routing and thermal analysis; a floorplan should not be called better for a physical flow if the later stages cannot legalize it or require more whitespace and repair.

Escaping Flatland: placement in a 3D FPGA is a placement-and-routing problem

What an FPGA placement actually chooses

An FPGA (a chip whose circuits can be rewired after it is made) contains programmable logic blocks and a programmable network of wires and switches. Placement assigns each logic block to a legal site.

Routing then selects the programmable wires and switch settings that connect the placed blocks. A placement is useful only when the later routing step can connect every required signal within the available resources.

A 3D FPGA adds a tier choice to the site choice. A logic block is assigned both an in-tier location and a layer.

Signals that remain on one layer use that layer's routing fabric. Signals that cross layers use vertical connections and the horizontal resources that reach them.

Those vertical connections are finite resources with their own delay, capacitance, area and congestion; they are not an unlimited shortcut between any two points.

This makes geometric distance an incomplete objective. Moving two connected blocks onto different tiers may reduce horizontal distance, but it can add a vertical crossing, consume a scarce connection, or force other nets through a congested region.

The relevant cost is the delay and resource demand of the complete routed path. A placement cost that represents only 2D wirelength can therefore prefer an arrangement that later routes poorly.

What the reported flow changes

The captured description introduces four changes aimed at this 3D-specific search: partitioning-based initialization, adaptive cost scheduling, refined delay estimation and a simulated-annealing move set designed for 3D architectures. Partitioning provides an initial layer assignment.

Cost scheduling changes which placement costs dominate at different stages. Delay estimation attempts to represent the timing effect of 3D connections during placement.

The move set lets the search explore layer assignments as well as ordinary site movements.

The source compares the flow with Verilog-To-Routing (VTR) on four named architecture families: 3D CB, 3D CB-O, 3D CB-I and 3D SB. It reports approximate geometric-mean and maximum reductions in critical-path delay and routed wirelength.

These are different summaries: a geometric mean describes typical multiplicative behavior across the tested cases, while a maximum identifies the strongest reported case. The maximum must not be read as the expected result for every circuit.

The evidence supports a placement flow that uses 3D-aware costs and layer moves. It does not identify the benchmark circuits, tier topology, vertical-link model, routing-capacity assumptions, runtime or physical FPGA implementation.

The description says the experiments include routed wirelength, but it does not provide enough detail to determine whether all comparisons used equal routing budgets, identical architecture parameters and successful routing for every case.

Clock analysis is part of the physical problem

A clock network distributes the timing signal used by storage elements. Differences in arrival time affect how much time data has to travel between them.

If two clock paths share a segment, independently assigning the shared segment its fastest and slowest delays can introduce an unnecessarily pessimistic comparison. Common-path pessimism removal accounts for that shared-path variation in timing analysis; it does not physically remove clock delay. Intel's timing-analysis documentation explains this distinction.

Flexicts incorporates that analysis into three-dimensional clock-tree synthesis. The unanswered issue is how its constructed network performs across layer crossings and variation conditions. The captured record has no clock-arrival difference, power, area, or final timing numbers.

FlexiCTS: clock-tree construction must preserve the timing paths that CPPR analyzes

What a clock tree and CPPR measure

A clock tree distributes one clock source to many storage elements. The useful property is not just that every endpoint receives a clock.

The clock must arrive within an acceptable time window at each endpoint and with acceptable differences between endpoints. Those arrival differences are clock skew.

Clock-tree synthesis chooses buffers, branching points, wire segments and, in a 3D design, the die or tier on which each part is placed.

Static timing analysis compares a launch clock path and a capture clock path. The two paths can share a physical segment before they separate.

If analysis treats the shared segment as having an independently worst delay in both comparisons, it can count the same uncertainty twice. Common-path pessimism removal (CPPR) removes that analysis over-counting for the shared portion.

CPPR changes the reported timing margin; it does not shorten the shared wire, improve the buffer, or remove variation in the manufactured clock network.

Face-to-face bonding makes the construction problem discrete and resource-constrained. A clock branch may stay on one die, cross to the other die, or share part of a path before crossing.

A cross-die branch consumes a hybrid-bonding terminal (HBT) or other vertical connection and adds the delay, capacitance, resistance and variation of that connection. If a flow partitions clock paths rigidly by die, it can destroy useful common paths and leave HBT locations poorly allocated.

The best CPPR result therefore depends on the actual tree topology and on whether the physical bond resources can support it.

What FlexiCTS changes

The captured description presents FlexiCTS as a CPPR-aware, correct-by-construction 3D clock-tree synthesis framework. Its adaptive cross-die buffer assignment decides where buffers are placed relative to die crossings so that path sharing can be preserved while HBT use is allocated.

“Correct by construction” is a property of the method's stated constraints; it is not evidence that a fabricated clock tree meets every signoff corner.

The source contrasts the approach with 2D clock-tree synthesis and CPPR-oblivious 3D CTS, and reports a claimed 4.1× skew reduction and 88% fewer HBTs than state-of-the-art methods while matching 2D timing quality. The record does not provide absolute skew, clock latency (the wait for one result), HBT counts, power, area, variation corners, benchmark designs, runtime or the definition of “matching.” A reduction factor without the baseline and endpoint values cannot show whether the remaining skew meets a required clock period.

The evaluation that would establish a usable 3D clock flow

Report the complete die topology, clock sinks, buffer library, HBT capacity and placement rules. Give absolute skew, insertion delay, transition time, area, power, HBT count and timing margin before and after CPPR, with the same constraints for every baseline.

Evaluate process, voltage, temperature, bonding-alignment and inter-die-variation corners. Compare the timing analysis estimate with extracted post-route timing and show whether CPPR changes only the pessimism calculation or also corresponds to a physically improved clock network.

Temperature must be connected to useful operation

3D eFlash analog IMC: thermal stability is a chain from temperature to task output

Start with the physical stack

The described system places a 12 nm logic die and a 28 nm eFlash die in a hybrid-bonded wafer-on-wafer stack. The eFlash die is reported to provide 36 MB for analog in-memory computing.

The process nodes identify fabrication generations; they do not by themselves determine thermal resistance, maximum temperature, memory programming behavior, analog noise, or system throughput (how much work is finished per second).

In analog in-memory computing, stored conductance or another physical state represents a weight, and an electrical operation in the memory array contributes to a computation. Temperature can change the stored state, the read/write response, the analog signal, and surrounding circuits.

A thermal claim therefore needs a chain of evidence: temperature distribution under a power pattern, change in stored or computed values, calibration or programming response, and final task output.

What the thermal-stability strategy changes

The source describes three cross-layer mechanisms. A physics-guided thermal model predicts temperature behavior using a model constrained by thermal relationships. The reported KL divergence of 0.088 is meaningful only with the reference distribution, normalization, operating points, and spatial/temporal aggregation defined.

Temperature-insensitive differential programming (TIDP) is intended to stabilize stored weights from −30°C to 60°C. Differential representation can reduce the effect of a common change, but it does not remove every temperature-dependent effect: the two programmed states can respond differently, read circuitry can drift, and programming itself may have temperature-dependent error.

The programming range is also not the same as the later task-evaluation range.

Adaptive temperature calibration (ATC) adjusts the computation or its interpretation using temperature information. The reported 60.7% reduction in output-error standard deviation must be tied to the baseline calibration, sensor conditions, calibration cost, update frequency, and remaining task error.

Calibration can improve output stability while consuming time, energy, memory, or control bandwidth (how many bytes a link can move per second).

The official description reports a first 3D eFlash analog-IMC prototype with hybrid bonding, less than 10% degradation in end-to-end task accuracy for ResNet-18 and DCCRN across −15°C to 75°C, and the model/error values above. It does not identify datasets, exact accuracy baseline, temperature measurement locations, power/cooling conditions, calibration overhead, endurance, retention, or silicon characterization protocol.

The source supports a prototype and a cross-layer strategy claim, not commercial qualification or universal thermal resilience. Source: official DAC description and captured record.

The evaluation that would establish thermal stability

Measure temperature at both dies and relevant array/periphery regions under repeatable workloads and cooling. Sweep the complete operating range, including the gap between the −30°C to 60°C programming claim and the −15°C to 75°C task claim.

Report raw weight drift, analog output error, calibration time/energy, task accuracy, throughput, endurance and retention before and after calibration. Compare TIDP and ATC separately against ordinary programming and calibration, and report whether the result is measured silicon, calibrated simulation, or a mixture.

The 12nm/28nm eFlash computing stack introduces a thermal-stability strategy for bonded memory and computation. The detailed explanation above separates process labels from thermal behavior, temperature from stored-weight/output error, programming range from task range, and prototype evidence from qualification; this card is the compact evidence record.

Taken together, these records motivate evaluating early design choices through completed physical implementation. They do not yet show which method wins.

A defensible comparison needs the same functional design, bonding limits, cooling conditions, connection resources, and timing requirements, with area and runtime counted after necessary repairs.

Compare the results carefully

How to compare these papers’ results fairly

First identify the inputs and required output quality. Then identify the limited resource: time, memory, communication, energy, circuit area, or correctness coverage.

Ask which part of the method reduces that cost and which new cost it adds. Compare numbers only when the experiments hold these conditions sufficiently similar.
